## In one line

The altitude a mission flies at fixes how fast its spacecraft moves and how long one orbit takes — higher is slower, and slower round a bigger circle is a longer orbit.

## Said simply

A spacecraft in orbit is falling all the time. It moves sideways so fast that, as it falls, the Earth curves away beneath it, and it never comes down. At each height there is exactly one sideways speed for which the fall matches the curve: that is the circular-orbit speed {{node orbit_velocity}}.

Higher up, gravity is weaker, so less speed is needed to bend the path into a circle — and the circle is bigger. Less speed round a longer path means each orbit takes longer {{node orbit_period}}.

## Where it breaks

Real orbits are not exact circles, the Earth is not a perfect sphere, and below about 300 km the air slows the spacecraft every orbit. These nodes give the ideal circle; drag and the Earth's flattening belong to other groups, which start from these numbers.

## Common misreading

"There is no gravity in orbit." There is: at 400 km it is still about 88% of its strength on the ground. Astronauts float because they and their spacecraft are falling together, not because gravity has gone.
