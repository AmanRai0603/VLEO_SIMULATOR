## Equations

```
dKp_peak(Ap) = piecewise_linear(ap_bin_centres -> measured_medians, Ap)
```

## Derivation

The previous row establishes by Jensen's inequality that reading the published table with a daily mean must under-read the day's worst three-hourly Kp. An inequality gives the sign and not the size, and a drag design needs the size: the atmosphere responds to the disturbance it actually met, which for a heating calculation is closer to the peak slot than to the daily average. So the size has to be measured, and it cannot be derived, because it depends on how unevenly a real day distributes its disturbance among its eight slots — which is a property of storms rather than of the table.

1. Define the quantity to be measured as the gap between what a design reads and what the day actually contained: the day's worst three-hourly Kp minus the table's reading of the day's mean Ap. `dKp_peak(day) = max_8 Kp(day) - Kp_table(Ap(day))`
2. Its sign is known in advance. Kp(ap) is concave, so the table's reading of a mean cannot exceed the largest of the values averaged — which means this difference cannot be negative, and a negative answer would say the sign or the slot had been swapped rather than that a quiet day had occurred.
3. It is measured as a function of Ap and not as a single number, because the unevenness grows with activity: a quiet day is uniformly quiet across its eight slots while a storm is concentrated in a few. Nine bins, the study's prf_ap2kp edges, and the median in each. `edges = 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 45, 70, 110, 400`
4. The medians rise with Ap as the concavity argument predicts — +1.000 at Ap 2.5 through +1.747 at Ap 90 — and then FALL to +1.317 in the 110-to-400 bin.
5. That fall is not smoothed away, and it is not defended either. The top bin holds 20 days, exactly prf_ap2kp's own minimum for using a bin at all, so the drop is as likely to be a small-sample artefact as a real saturation of the table near its top. Smoothing it would be this row inventing a shape the record does not show; anyone designing above Ap 110 is reading a correction supported by twenty days.
6. Between bin centres the answer is interpolated and at the ends it is held rather than extrapolated, which is prf_ap2kp's own rule stated explicitly: hold the end bins, never extrapolate.

## Assumptions

- The correction is a median per bin, so it describes the typical day at that Ap and not the day in hand. Fails when: the offset is a distribution, not a number. Adding the median recovers the typical peak and still misses any individual day, and the spread within a bin is not published by this row. A design that needs the worst case at a given Ap needs a percentile of the offset, not its median.
- The nine bin medians are not monotone, and the top bin sits exactly at the record's support limit. Fails when: the offset rises with Ap as the concavity argument predicts — +1.000 at Ap 2.5 through +1.747 at Ap 90 — and then FALLS to +1.317 in the 110-to-400 bin. That bin holds 20 days, which is exactly prf_ap2kp's own minimum for using a bin at all, so the fall is as likely to be a small-sample artefact as a real saturation of the table near its top. It is carried through rather than smoothed away, because smoothing it would be this node inventing a shape the record does not show. Anyone designing at Ap above 110 is reading a correction supported by twenty days.
- The 24-hour-mean slot is NOT what this publishes. Fails when: the two slots have opposite signs, and using this row for the mean slot would double the error rather than remove it. Measured on the same 10,297 days, the mean-slot offset runs from +0.042 at Ap 2.5 down to -0.487 in the top bin — the table reads HIGH against the 24-hour mean and LOW against the peak. DTM2020_Oper wants both: akp(1) is a single three-hourly value, akp(3) the mean of the eight. The mean-slot offset is published by sw_kp_mean_bias; this row is the peak.
- WHICH SLOT A DESIGN IS DRIVEN BY IS NOT THIS ROW'S TO SAY, AND sw_kp_driving_slot IS WHERE IT WILL BE SAID. Fails when: this row is read as the answer to which slot matters. It corrects the table for ONE slot, the daily peak, and its existence is evidence that the two slots differ — not a statement that the peak is the one a design is driven by. That statement is sw_kp_driving_slot, which is seeded and unanswered: §30 B1 asks for it and the value needs a person. Until it carries one, every consumer of a Kp in this tree is choosing a slot implicitly, and this row and sw_kp_mean_bias exist to correct whichever one it turns out to be. The cross-reference is here so the two cannot drift: if sw_kp_driving_slot is answered 'mean', it is sw_kp_mean_bias and not this row that a drag design reads, and §43 measures what that choice is worth downstream — 70 rows move at either scenario, six of them KPI closures, and tools/kp_slot_cost.py re-measures it on every run

## Validity

From 0 to 2 One. Below: Kp(ap) is concave, so by Jensen's inequality the daily peak three-hourly Kp cannot sit below the table's reading of the daily mean. A negative correction contradicts the inequality the whole row rests on, and means the sign or the slot has been swapped. The smallest measured bin median is +0.833. Above: the largest measured bin median is +1.747 and the relation is a table that clamps at its ends, so no input can produce more than that. 2.0 is therefore unreachable by this relation and exists to catch a broken table rather than an extreme sky.

A median per bin, so it recovers the typical day at that Ap and misses any individual one; the spread within a bin is not published here, and a design needing a worst case at a given Ap needs a percentile of the offset rather than its median. It is also the PEAK-slot offset only. The 24-hour-mean slot has its own bias of the opposite sign — running from +0.042 down to -0.487 — so applying this row to the mean slot would double the error rather than remove it. Models like DTM2020 want both kinds of input, and both are now published: the mean-slot offset is sw_kp_mean_bias.
