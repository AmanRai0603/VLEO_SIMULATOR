## Said simply

How wrong a forecast of the Sun's flux would be if it simply assumed today's value continues, at a given lead. The error grows from about 7 solar flux units at one day to about 50 at two years, with a dip at 27 days, because the Sun turns and the same active region faces Earth again.

## Picture it

Guess tomorrow's weather by saying "same as today" and you will usually be close; try it a fortnight ahead and you will often be wrong. The Sun adds a twist, because it turns like a lighthouse lamp: a bright active region facing us today comes round to face us again about 27 days later. So "same as today" is a better guess one rotation ahead than half a rotation ahead.

## Where it breaks

In a real run it is only ever asked about leads of half a year and up, so the dip and the short-lead shape are carried but never used. The error has not levelled off even at two years, so reading the tail as settled understates how much worse longer leads get.

## Common misreading

It is natural to assume forecast error only rises with lead. Today's flux is a better guide to one rotation ahead than to a fortnight ahead, and a smooth model would erase that.
