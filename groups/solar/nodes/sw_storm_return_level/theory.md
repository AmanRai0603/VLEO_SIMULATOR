## Equations

```
Ap(T) = 92.5155 + 40.9265*ln(T / 1 yr)
```

## Derivation

Geomagnetic activity has no usable forecast at mission timescales, so a design cannot predict the storm it will meet. What it can do is size for the worst storm the mission is LIKELY to meet, which turns the mission length into the input: a five-year mission and a fifteen-year one are exposed to different extremes of the same distribution. That is extreme-value reasoning rather than forecasting, and the whole row is the empirical exceedance curve of the daily record with a functional form fitted through its tail.

1. Return period is not a model, it is a definition: the average interval between events that exceed a level, which is the reciprocal of the exceedance probability per unit time. Inverting it turns 'how long is the mission' into 'how rare a storm must it tolerate'. `T(x) = 1 / P(Ap > x)`
2. The empirical curve comes straight from the ranked record with nothing assumed. Sort the daily Ap descending; the m-th largest value has been exceeded m times in the record, so its return period is the record's length divided by m. `T(rank m) = 28.1971 yr / m`
3. That gives a staircase rather than a function, and a design needs a value at an arbitrary mission length, so a form has to be fitted through it. Which form is not arbitrary — it is a statement about the shape of the tail, and the two candidates make different claims.
4. Log-linear in the return period is exactly the claim that the upper tail is EXPONENTIAL. If the probability of exceeding x falls off as exp(-x/b), then the return period rises as exp(x/b), and inverting that gives the level growing with the logarithm of the return period. `P(Ap > x) ~ exp(-x/b) => T ~ exp(x/b) => x = a + b*ln(T)`
5. Fitted over ranks 2 to 56 — return periods 0.5035 to 14.0986 years — least squares gives the two coefficients. The residual rms against the empirical staircase is 4.95 Ap, worst +8.6 and -12.9. `Ap(T) = 92.5155 + 40.9265 * ln(T / 1 yr)`
6. A power law was tried on the same points and is more than twice as bad — rms 9.10, worst -47.7 — which is the evidence for the exponential tail rather than a heavier one. That is a claim about this record over this range of return periods and not about geomagnetic storms in general.
7. Rank 1 is deliberately excluded from the fitting range. The record's largest day, Ap 273, sits above the log-linear trend and has an apparent return period of exactly 28.2 years for no reason except that it is the largest thing in 28.2 years. Including it would let one day set the slope of the whole tail.
8. At the declared five-year mission the curve returns 158.4 Ap, and the whole design chain hangs off that: it is what sw_kp_from_ap converts, what l3_solar_ach_03 publishes as the achieved level, and what the requirement at 150 fails against. `Ap(5 yr) = 92.5155 + 40.9265 * ln(5) = 158.38`
9. One assumption underneath all of this is worth naming because it is not visible in the algebra: every day is treated as an independent draw. Storms cluster — a coronal hole returns each solar rotation and a single event runs for days — so the record holds fewer independent storms than storm days. Clustering does not bias a quantile of the marginal distribution, but it does mean the effective sample behind the tail is smaller than the day count and the true uncertainty is wider than the residual above suggests.

## Assumptions

- The tail is log-linear in the return period, with the two coefficients fitted on this record. Fails when: the form is a choice, not the source's. Fitted over ranks 2 to 56 of the record, which is return periods 0.5035 to 14.0986 years; it carries a residual rms of 4.95 Ap against the empirical curve, worst +8.6 and -12.9. A power law on the same points is more than twice as bad (rms 9.10, worst -47.7), which is why this form and not that one. Anyone who needs the empirical step rather than a smooth curve should read the record, not this row.
- 28.2 years of record support the whole curve, and its top end rests on two observations. Fails when: the empirical method cannot see past its own record length. At T = 15 years the answer is fitted through the second-largest daily Ap in 28.2 years, and at T = 10 years the third; the record's largest value, Ap 273, has an apparent return period of exactly 28.2 years for no reason other than that it is the largest thing in 28.2 years. The fitted domain is 0.5035 to 14.0986 years, and the declared input range 0.5 to 15 years reaches past BOTH ends of it: the curve is extrapolated by 0.285 Ap at a half-year mission and by 2.54 Ap at a fifteen-year one, because there is no rank between 1 and 2 and rank 1 is the record length itself. Small, but it is an extrapolation and an earlier version of this sheet claimed it was never one. A mission at the 15-year bound is being sized on a curve whose top is two data points. A design that needs the once-per-century storm needs a longer record or a fitted extreme-value model, not this row.
- Every day in the record is treated as an independent draw. Fails when: storms cluster — a coronal hole returns once per solar rotation and a single event runs for more than one day — so the record holds fewer independent storms than it holds storm days. Clustering does not bias the exceedance level itself, which is a quantile of the marginal distribution, but it does mean the effective sample behind the tail is smaller than N and the uncertainty on the answer is wider than the residual above suggests. sw_event_duration and sw_recurrence_lag both measure the clustering now — a mean disturbed run of 3.31 days and a recurrence peak at a lag of 26 days — and neither is fed back into this fit, so the widening they imply is declared here rather than applied.
- The record this is fitted on is missing 273 days, and the missing window contains storms. Fails when: 2017-01-01 to 2017-09-30 has no rows in the daily table — nine months absent from a 29-year span, and the node's answer is a tail quantile of what remains. The hole is not empty of weather: the same bundle's alerts.csv records four days inside it with an OBSERVED K of 7 or more, including K = 8 three times on 7-8 September 2017. Bounding those days from the alerts (their other three-hourly intervals held at the record median) puts 2017-09-08 at a daily Ap of at least 104 and 2017-09-07 at 48, which would rank about 24th and 43rd in the record. Restoring them moves the five-year level by -0.0 Ap and the fifteen-year by -0.7: the tail is set by events far larger, so this particular gap does not bias this particular answer. A different gap, or one holding a top-ten storm, would.
- ap_planetary is the SWPC estimated planetary A, not the GFZ definitive value. Fails when: anyone reconciling these return levels against GFZ's definitive Ap series will find differences, and they are not transcription errors — they are two different published quantities. The bundle's INDEX.md says so; it is repeated here because a node author reads the sheet and not necessarily the bundle.

## Validity

From 20 to 230 One. Below: ap is an equivalent amplitude in nanotesla. Below 20 the answer is not a storm at all — the record's median day is 7 — so a return level under it means the input or the fit reached somewhere neither was meant to go. Above: THE BOUND IS THE RECORD'S LENGTH, NOT THE TABLE'S END. The fit evaluated at a return period equal to the record itself, 28.1971 years, is Ap 229.18; 230 is the first round number above it, so a return period beyond the record refuses instead of answering. The previous bound was 400 — the last point of the published ap/Kp table — which first bites at a return period of 1832 years, sixty-five times the record, and so enforced nothing: this node would answer a once-per-century question with Ap 281 while its own assumptions said a century needs a longer record. Note that 230 is BELOW the record's largest single day, Ap 273: rank 1 sits above the log-linear trend and was deliberately left out of the fitting range, so the fit does not chase it. This bound is the fit's own reach, not the record's extreme.

28.2 years of record support the whole curve and its top end rests on two observations. At a fifteen-year mission the answer is fitted through the second-largest daily Ap in the record; at ten years, the third. The declared input range of 0.5 to 15 years reaches past both ends of the fitted range, 0.5035 to 14.0986 years, by 0.285 Ap at the bottom and 2.54 Ap at the top — small, and an extrapolation nonetheless. A design needing the once-per-century storm needs a longer record or a fitted extreme-value model, not this row. The upper bound of 230 is the fit evaluated at a return period equal to the record itself, so asking for a storm rarer than the record refuses rather than answering.
