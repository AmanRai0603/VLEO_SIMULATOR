## Said simply

How wrong a forecast of the Sun's flux would be if it always predicted the long-run average and ignored today. It is almost flat, about 44 to 45 solar flux units at every lead, and it is the yardstick for the other cheap forecast: today's flux beats it out to about 547 days and loses by 730.

## Picture it

Imagine a forecaster who never looks out of the window and always says "the average for the year". Their forecast for tomorrow is exactly as good as their forecast for next year, because neither uses anything about today. This row measures that forecaster, and their error stays flat because it is simply how far real days scatter from the average.

## Where it breaks

This baseline knows nothing about where the Sun is in its cycle; one that did would be much better and would beat today's-flux sooner, so the horizon it implies is too long. The crossover itself is not published by this row, and the table must be re-measured if the data bundle changes.

## Common misreading

It is easy to expect a baseline error that grows with lead. This one ignores lead, so its error is just the spread of the record about its mean, and that flatness is the point.
