The thermosphere is heated from above and from the side, and those are different mechanisms on different timescales, so the temperature it settles at is a sum of two terms rather than a function of one driver.

From above: solar extreme ultraviolet, absorbed at 100 to 200 km, is what sets the base level. EUV is not measured continuously from the ground, so a proxy stands in for it, and the 10.7 cm radio flux is the one with the longest uninterrupted record — it comes from the same active regions that emit the EUV without being the EUV. That proxy relationship is why every term here is a fitted coefficient and not a physical constant.

From the side: geomagnetic activity drives currents in the auroral electrojets and the Joule dissipation of those currents deposits energy directly into the thermosphere at high latitude. Kp indexes that, and its effect is not linear in Kp because Kp is itself a quasi-logarithmic scale — which is why an exponential appears in a relation that is otherwise straight lines.

`T_inf` is the asymptotic temperature the profile approaches with height, not the temperature anywhere in particular. It is the single number a Bates profile needs to give a temperature at every altitude, which is what `env::temperature` does with it next.

  1. Start from the base level with no Sun and no storm. 379 K is the intercept of the fit, and it is not a physical floor — the declared lower bound of 400 K is, because below that the Bates profile this feeds inverts.T_inf(0, 0, 0) = 379 K
  2. Add the slow solar term. The 81-day centred mean of the 10.7 cm flux stands in for the EUV averaged over three solar rotations, which is the timescale the whole thermosphere equilibrates on. This is the dominant term: over the observed range of F10.7A it moves T_inf by about 900 K.+ 3.24 * F10.7A
  3. Add the fast solar term, and note that it is a DEPARTURE and not a level. What enters is today's flux minus its own 81-day mean, so a day at its mean contributes nothing. The coefficient is smaller than the slow one — 1.3 against 3.24 — because the thermosphere cannot fully respond to a single day, and a relation that used one coefficient for both would be claiming it could.+ 1.3 * (F10.7 - F10.7A)
  4. Add the geomagnetic term, in two parts because Kp is a quasi-logarithmic index. The linear part carries the ordinary response and the exponential carries the storm tail: at Kp 3 the exponential contributes 0.6 K of 84.6, and at Kp 7 it contributes 32.9 K of 228.9. A relation with only the linear part would be right at quiet levels and would under-read every storm.+ 28 * Kp + 0.03 * exp(Kp)

Reading the answer

It is a NIGHT-TIME MINIMUM in a global average: the coolest the thermosphere gets at that level of activity, with no time of day and no time of year in it. The diurnal bulge adds up to about 30 per cent at 14:00 local solar time, so a design sized on this number alone is sized on the quiet side of the day, and a vehicle in a dawn-dusk orbit and one in a noon-midnight orbit are given the same answer.

It is also a FIT, over a record that ended in the 1960s and 70s. Every coefficient here was regressed against satellite drag data of that era, so the relation carries that era's instrumentation and that era's solar cycles. Later models — MSIS, DTM, JB2008 — exist because this one has known biases, and the reason to use it anyway is that its drivers are the three this tree computes and theirs are not.

which is the relation this node states: T_inf = 379 + 3.24*F10.7A + 1.3*(F10.7 - F10.7A) + 28*Kp + 0.03*exp(Kp)
