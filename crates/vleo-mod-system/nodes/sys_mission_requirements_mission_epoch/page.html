On what date does the mission begin?

T_epoch = 9862 d (2027-01-01)

Source orbitt_case_c1 — Orbitt Space — VLEO multipayload reference case C1 internal, 2026-09-01

The date every dated question in the design keys off. It is a mission requirement and it lives here, at the system layer, because the mission owns it and several subsystems read it — the solar-weather subsystem is the first to do so. Carried as days since 2000-01-01 so that nothing downstream needs a calendar.

fails when the record runs to 2025-12-31, day 9496, and this epoch is day 9862. So every row that reads it is answering about a date the record does not cover, by extrapolating a pattern rather than reading an observation. That is the correct thing to do for a design — a mission in the future has no record — and it means the cycle-phase rows fold the phase using a mean cycle length instead of measuring one. Which is exactly what prf_design does beyond its last cycle, and is declared on each row that does it.

There is no theory of a date. What there is, and what belongs here, is the argument for why a design needs one at all, and why this one. The environment a vehicle flies through is not stationary: solar activity runs on a cycle of about eleven years, and the same orbit flown in 2020 and in 2027 meets fluxes that differ by more than a factor of two. Until a date exists, the best any environment row can do is the unconditional statistics of the whole record — the average of every part of every cycle, which is a sky no mission flies through. Every dated question in the design then has to key off the SAME date, or the subsystems are each designing for a different mission.

  1. Start from what makes a date load-bearing. Solar activity is cyclical, so the environment is a function of when. The record's F10.7 runs from 64 to 343 sfu, and the phase-stacked mean of a cycle runs from 72 to 165: choosing a date chooses which half of that range the design is sized against.
  2. A date shared by several subsystems is not a subsystem's property. If the solar rows and the thermal rows each held their own epoch, the two would drift apart in the first revision and nothing would detect it. So the row sits at layer 2, the system layer, where the mission's own requirements live, and the subsystems read it.
  3. A calendar date is not arithmetic. Months have different lengths, leap years are irregular, and a row that had to know about either would carry a calendar. Counting days from a fixed origin makes every dated question a subtraction, and 2000-01-01 is the origin the engine's tables already use.T = days since 2000-01-01; 2027-01-01 -> 9862 d
  4. Now the choice itself. Cycle 25 opened on 2019-12-01 and the mean length of the two complete cycles in the record is 11.44 years, so this epoch folds to cycle phase 0.6194 — past maximum, on the declining side.phase = (9862 - 7274) / (11.44 * 365.25) = 0.6194
  5. That is the phase band the design's own exceedance statistics point at. sw_exceedance_phase measures the median cycle phase of the days that exceed the design Ap at 0.603, with a spread of 0.29 to 0.74. The declared epoch sits inside that band, which means it is a date chosen to face the storms rather than one chosen to avoid them.
  6. It is also 366 days past the end of the record, which stops at 2025-12-31. So every row that reads it is answering about a date nothing observed. That is the correct position for a design — a mission in the future has no record — and it is why the cycle rows fold a phase with a mean length instead of measuring one, and why each of them declares that it does.

Reading the answer

It is an instant, not a duration, and it crosses the interface in seconds like every other Time — 852076800 s for day 9862. Nothing downstream should do calendar arithmetic on it. The number is a decision and not a measurement, so the honest use of it is to move it: the cycle-dependent half of the subsystem answers differently at a different epoch, and a launch window rather than a launch date means running the design at both ends of the window and keeping the worse answer.
